Growing a ligand out of fragments

ShEPhERD-2 · bioisosteric fragment merging · interactive

ShEPhERD-2 doesn't generate a molecule directly. It generates a molecule and the interaction profile that molecule presents to a protein — its electrostatic surface and its pharmacophores — jointly, in one reverse-diffusion process. That makes a particular kind of design problem tractable: you can hand it an interaction profile assembled from pieces, and ask for one molecule that satisfies all of it.

Below is that idea run end to end against the D68 3C protease. Several fragments were soaked into the same binding site; two of them are merged into one generated molecule, and that product is then merged with a third. Walk the seven stages with the timeline under the viewer. Everything sits in the crystal frame throughout, so nothing is realigned between stages — the overlays line up because they genuinely do.

Drag to rotate, scroll to zoom. Stages 3 and 6 are the generation trajectories: their chip opens into a slider you can scrub, pause and step through with the arrow keys.

Carbons are coloured by which structure an atom belongs to; heteroatoms keep their usual CPK colours in every structure, which is what makes the overlays comparable. Hydrogens are hidden by default — the toggle brings them back. Each generation runs 400 denoising steps, played back at 121 sampled frames.

What each stage shows

  1. Fragments bound. Three fragments from a crystallographic screen, each sitting in the same pocket and each making only a handful of contacts. The ribbon is the apo protein; it fades out with distance from the site so it stays context rather than clutter.
  2. Pick 8 and 10. These two occupy neighbouring subsites. Their interaction profiles — the ESP surface and the typed pharmacophores — are what gets handed to the model, not their atoms.
  3. Generate the merge. The joint reverse diffusion, conditioned on the combined profile. Bonds are drawn from interatomic distances rather than predicted, which is why they flicker early and only lock in once the geometry is chemically sensible; the atom count moves too, because composition is diffused along with coordinates.
  4. Merge vs. 8 + 10. The product over the two fragments it came from. Note that it isn't a linker bridging them — it's a single scaffold that happens to land the same contacts.
  5. Bring in 12. A third fragment occupying a subsite the round-one molecule doesn't reach.
  6. Generate round 2. The round-one product goes back in as input, together with fragment 12's profile.
  7. Final molecule. One molecule over all three starting fragments.

The three channels

x1 is the molecule itself: atom positions and identities. x3 is the electrostatic potential sampled on a shell around it — blue positive, red negative. x4 is the pharmacophore set: typed interaction points with direction vectors where direction matters (donors, acceptors, aromatic ring normals). x4 carries most of the conditioning signal; it is what lets you ask for a molecule that makes a specific set of contacts without specifying the molecule.

The Interaction profile toggle shows x3 and x4 for whichever structures the current stage is about. It comes on by itself at the stages where the profile is the point, and off at the overlay stages, where the atoms are.
